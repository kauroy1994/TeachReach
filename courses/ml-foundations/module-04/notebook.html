Whose next score will we predict?

A population is the group or process we want to predict for. A sample is the set of records we actually observe. Define the prediction time too. Information available after the exam cannot help a student making a prediction before it.

Worked example

Imagine a tiny population of four equally likely study records. Hours and scores are (0,50), (1,56), (2,60), and (3,66). The third record reuses Ava’s hours and score. We use just hours here to compare rules by hand. In this toy world we know every score. Usually we see only a sample.

Formal notation

X denotes the input, here hours, and Y denotes the observed score. A row (x, y) gives one particular input and result.

rows = [(0, 50), (1, 56), (2, 60), (3, 66)]
sample = [rows[2]]
print("population size", len(rows))
print("sample", sample)
Reveal the output
population size 4
sample [(2, 60)]

Check your understanding

If the intended population changes to all students taking a different exam, can we still treat the four rows as its full population?

Reveal answer

No. The four records describe the invented exam. A new exam could change both which students appear and how scores relate to preparation.

Two rules can agree on Ava

A hypothesis is a candidate prediction rule. A hypothesis class is the set of rules we agree to consider. Restricting the class makes some patterns easier to learn and others impossible to express. That preference is an inductive bias.

Worked example

Consider two candidates. One always predicts 60. The other starts at 50 and adds 5 points per hour. Both predict 60 for Ava at two hours. At zero hours they differ by 10 points.

Formal notation

H = {f, g} is a set of two rules. f(x) = 60 and g(x) = 50 + 5x. The notation f(x) means the number that rule f returns at input x.

for hours in (2, 0):
    print(hours, 60, 50 + 5 * hours)
Reveal the output
2 60 60
0 60 50

Check your understanding

If the training sample contains only Ava, can squared error choose between these rules?

Reveal answer

Both predictions equal 60. Each has zero training error. A preference between them would have to come from another observation or an assumption about the pattern.

Turn individual misses into a sample average

Define a loss for one prediction, then average the losses. Squared loss counts a miss twice as large as four times as costly. The mean squared loss on the observed data is called empirical risk.

Worked example

For sample rows (0,50) and (2,60), the constant-60 rule misses by 10 and 0 points. Squaring gives 100 and 0. Their mean is 50 squared points.

Formal notation

ℓ(ŷ, y) = (ŷ − y)². Here ŷ is the prediction. R̂(f) = (1/n) Σᵢ ℓ(f(xᵢ), yᵢ). The index i visits each of the n sample rows once, and Σ means add.

import torch
scores = torch.tensor([50., 60.])
errors = 60 - scores
print(errors.square().tolist())
print(errors.square().mean().item())
Reveal the output
[100.0, 0.0]
50.0

Check your understanding

The sample contains scores 56 and 66. What is the empirical squared risk of predicting 60 for both?

Reveal answer

The errors are 4 and −6. Average the squares to obtain (16 + 36)/2 = 26 squared points.

Calculate the risk for the whole toy population

Expected risk means the average loss over future cases from the chosen population. A common case contributes more than a rare case. With four equally likely records, each contributes one quarter of its loss.

Worked example

The constant rule gives losses 100, 16, 0, 36. Its population risk is 38. The line gives losses 0, 1, 0, 1. Its risk is 0.5. Ava alone gave zero for both.

Formal notation

R(f) = E[ℓ(f(X), Y)]. E means average using the population probabilities. For discrete cases with probabilities pᵢ, multiply each loss by pᵢ and sum.

rows = [(0,50), (1,56), (2,60), (3,66)]
for name, predict in [("constant", lambda x: 60),
                      ("line", lambda x: 50 + 5*x)]:
    losses = [(predict(x)-y)**2 for x,y in rows]
    print(name, losses, sum(losses)/len(rows))
Reveal the output
constant [100, 16, 0, 36] 38.0
line [0, 1, 0, 1] 0.5

Check your understanding

Keep the four scores fixed but make Ava the only case that ever occurs. What is each rule’s expected risk?

Reveal answer

The population now puts probability 1 on Ava and 0 elsewhere. Both rules predict Ava exactly, so both expected risks are zero.

Why estimates vary between samples

An estimator is a recipe that converts sample data into a number. Its result can change when the sample changes. We can see that variation exactly by enumerating every possible pair of distinct students in this small population.

Worked example

For the constant rule, one pair gives a risk estimate of (100 + 16)/2 = 58. Another gives (0 + 36)/2 = 18. The rule did not change. Different students entered the estimate.

Formal notation

R̂ uses a sample. In this example every unordered pair has equal probability, so the mean over all six pair estimates equals the population risk R.

from itertools import combinations
losses = [100, 16, 0, 36]
estimates = [(a+b)/2 for a,b in combinations(losses, 2)]
print(estimates)
print("mean across pairs", sum(estimates)/len(estimates))
Reveal the output
[58.0, 50.0, 68.0, 8.0, 26.0, 18.0]
mean across pairs 38.0

Check your understanding

If we always sample only the two students with the largest hours, what estimate do we get for the constant rule?

Reveal answer

Their losses are 0 and 36, so the estimate is 18. Repeating the same selection gives 18 again, even though the equally weighted population risk is 38.

Separate choosing a rule from checking it

Use training data to fit parameters. If you compare design choices, use validation data to choose among them. Reserve test data for a final check after the choices are fixed. A test average estimates performance on similar future cases when the sampling design supports that interpretation.

Worked example

Let training contain hours 0 and 2, and let test contain hours 1 and 3. The line beats the constant on training, so select the line using training alone. Its mean test loss is 1. This two-row test set is a very small estimate.

Formal notation

f̂ = argmin over f in H of R̂train(f). Read argmin as “the candidate with the smallest value.” R̂test(f̂) then averages the already chosen rule’s losses on the test rows.

train = [(0,50), (2,60)]
test = [(1,56), (3,66)]
rules = {"constant": lambda x: 60, "line": lambda x: 50+5*x}
def risk(rule, rows):
    return sum((rule(x)-y)**2 for x,y in rows)/len(rows)
best = min(rules, key=lambda name: risk(rules[name], train))
print(best, risk(rules[best], train), risk(rules[best], test))
Reveal the output
line 0.0 1.0

Check your understanding

What goes wrong if you repeatedly choose a different rule after viewing the same test scores?

Reveal answer

The test results influence the rule. That set now helps select the model and can give an optimistic final estimate. An independent final evaluation needs fresh cases.

Spot information that arrives too late

Leakage occurs when training or evaluation uses information unavailable under the intended prediction conditions. A model can look accurate for the wrong reason. Checking when an input becomes known is as important as checking its numerical type.

Worked example

Suppose a column called adjusted_total equals the exam score plus two administrative points. Subtracting two gives perfect predictions in the stored table. At prediction time the exam has not happened, so the feature does not exist yet.

Formal notation

For an input x to support prediction before time t, every part of x must be available by t. A low test loss alone cannot establish that condition.

scores = [50, 56, 60, 66]
adjusted_totals = [score + 2 for score in scores]
leaked_predictions = [total - 2 for total in adjusted_totals]
print(sum((p-y)**2 for p,y in zip(leaked_predictions,scores))/len(scores))
Reveal the output
0.0

Check your understanding

You want to predict a new student’s performance, but that student’s duplicate record appears in training. How would you split the data?

Reveal answer

Keep rows for the same student together and split by student. This makes the test student genuinely new. If the goal also involves future exams, preserve the timing when you choose the split.

Turn Stage I into a learning procedure

A model needs a representation, a loss, a way to fit parameters, and a way to evaluate the fitted rule. Choosing among two hand-written rules was a first version of fitting. Stage II will let the examples determine a slope and intercept.

Worked example

Fit a constant predictor to scores 50, 56, 60. Among constants, the sample mean minimizes squared loss. Check three nearby constants. Moving from the optimum by one point raises the mean squared loss by exactly one.

Formal notation

For a constant c, the objective is the average of (c − yᵢ)². Its slope with respect to c is 2(c − ȳ), where ȳ is the mean score. The slope vanishes at c = ȳ, the bottom of this upward-opening curve.

import torch
scores = torch.tensor([50.,56.,60.], dtype=torch.float64)
center = scores.mean()
for shift in (-1,0,1):
    c = center + shift
    print(round(c.item(),3), round((c-scores).square().mean().item(),3))
Reveal the output
54.333 17.889
55.333 16.889
56.333 17.889

Check your understanding

What do hours contribute to the fitted constant prediction?

Reveal answer

Nothing. A class containing only constants treats all students alike. A line with a learned slope can use differences in hours.

Practice

Question 1

A constant prediction of 12 meets observations 10, 11, 15. Write and compute its empirical squared risk.

Hint

Subtract each observation, square, then average.

Reveal the worked answer

R̂(c) = [(c−10)²+(c−11)²+(c−15)²]/3. At c=12 the value is (4+1+9)/3 = 14/3 squared units.

Question 2

A rule loses 1 on ordinary cases and 9 on unusual cases. Future cases are 75% ordinary. Find expected loss and compare with a sample containing one of each.

Hint

Weight by how often each case occurs.

Reveal the worked answer

Population risk is 0.75×1+0.25×9=3. The balanced sample gives (1+9)/2=5 because unusual cases have a larger share in that sample.

Question 3

Two candidate rules predict 70 on every training record. At a new input they predict 65 and 85. What evidence would discriminate between them?

Hint

Look for a record on which their predictions differ.

Reveal the worked answer

A labeled case on which they differ lets us compare losses. More such independently sampled cases better represent which inputs matter. Agreement on training records alone cannot break the tie.

Question 4

You fit twenty models and report the smallest of twenty test errors on the same students. Redesign the evaluation.

Hint

Which set made the selection?

Reveal the worked answer

The so-called test set selected the winner. Fit on training data, choose among twenty using validation data, then evaluate that single choice on untouched students sampled for the prediction goal.

Question 5

A score predictor uses a retake invitation sent after grading. Its held-out error is tiny. What is the first check?

Hint

Write down the time at which the prediction is needed.

Reveal the worked answer

If the goal is a prediction before the exam, the invitation arrives too late. Remove it, rebuild training features as of prediction time, and rerun the evaluation.

Question 6

Two independent small samples give estimates 12 and 18. Does the difference alone show that one estimate was computed incorrectly?

Hint

What changes between the averages?

Reveal the worked answer

No. Different sampled cases may contribute different individual losses. Check the arithmetic, sample selection and variation before attributing the difference to a mistake.
