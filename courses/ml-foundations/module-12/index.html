How can a model score the next token?

After “the cat,” a toy model assigns 0.75 to “sat” and 0.25 to “ran.” The next token in the example is “ran.” The probability assigned to that observed token determines the training loss.

WHAT YOU'LL LEARN

Learning outcomes

  • Explain a next-token prediction objective.
  • Compare greedy, temperature, and top-k sampling.
  • Describe limitations of evaluation and the difference between training and adaptation.
HOW THIS MODULE IS TAUGHT

How this lesson builds

  1. Split a short text into tokens and shift the sequence by one position to create input–target pairs. Track which earlier tokens the model may read.
  2. Look up each observed target’s conditional probability. Multiply to score a short sequence or add negative logarithms to obtain its total loss.
  3. For two candidate tokens, compare always selecting the larger probability with sampling in proportion to both. Change temperature or restrict the choices to the top k and inspect the result.
  4. Distinguish large-scale next-token training from adaptation to a smaller collection. Evaluate on new text with per-token loss, then examine a plausible but factually wrong completion.

MODULE 12 · EXERCISES

Practice, then demonstrate independently

Try it on paper

After “the cat,” a toy model assigns 0.75 to “sat” and 0.25 to “ran.” The next token in the example is “ran.” The probability assigned to that observed token determines the training loss.

A model assigns conditional probabilities 0.5 and 0.25 to two successive observed tokens. What probability does it assign to the pair?

Hint

Use the second probability conditional on the first token.

Reveal answerHide answer

0.5 × 0.25 = 0.125. This multiplication uses conditional probabilities and requires no independence assumption.
