COURSE DESCRIPTION

Learn why machine learning works.

Students first learn how data and models are represented mathematically, then derive the statistical and computational principles behind prediction, estimation, optimization, and generalization. The course progresses through classical models, representation learning, transformers, language modeling, generative techniques, reinforcement learning, and responsible evaluation.

Each topic follows the same instructional arc: a concrete problem → visual intuition → formal mathematics → executable PyTorch → evaluation and critical reflection. The Campus Study Coach example connects the foundations, with additional task-appropriate datasets introduced as needed.

Instructor: Kaushik Roy, University of Alabama

DELIVERY & COURSE STRUCTURE

16 modules in four stages

  • Stage I (01–04): Mathematical Foundations
  • Stage II (05–08): Fundamental Learning Algorithms
  • Stage III (09–13): Foundations of Modern AI
  • Stage IV (14–16): Intelligent and Reliable Learning

Teaching resources are browser-accessible and designed to support classroom instruction and independent review. Every module ends with an instructor-administered, graded in-class exercise variant.

Modules 01–03 have complete learning materials. Modules 04–16 have syllabus previews. Assessment dates are announced by the instructor.

COURSE REQUIREMENTS

What students need to participate

Preparation

  • Comfort with high-school algebra, functions, and basic mathematical notation.
  • Basic Python programming: variables, conditionals, loops, functions, and simple arrays. Python preparation materials may be assigned before starting.
  • No prior machine-learning experience is required; linear algebra, probability, and gradients are taught in Stages I–II.
  • Willingness to explain intermediate reasoning—not just provide final answers or run code.

Technical environment

  • A laptop or desktop with an up-to-date browser and reliable access to the course website.
  • Access to Python and PyTorch; free Google Colab is sufficient for introductory exercises.
  • Modern deep-learning modules may optionally use cloud GPU access for demonstrations; required assessments should use manageable datasets and compute.
  • No paid language-model API or local GPU is required merely to access the course materials.

Required learning activities

  • Study each module's instructional slides and mathematical explanations.
  • Complete the module's formative exercises, visualize the core concepts, and work through its notebook.
  • Explain the model assumptions, calculations, and failure modes in clear language.
  • Participate in the end-of-module in-class assessment and complete the final integrated capstone.

Materials and feedback

  • Learning pages supply examples, guided hints, code, and module-specific reference explanations.
  • Formative exercise attempts are for preparation and may be repeated.
  • Graded questions use a new in-class variant rather than reusing the published practice exercise verbatim.
  • The instructor provides assessment guidance, release dates, and any permitted reference-material policy before each graded session.

EXERCISES, ASSESSMENTS & GRADING

Practice is formative. The end-of-module variant is graded.

How assessment works—every module

  1. Students study the relevant slides, examples, interactive lab, and PyTorch notebook.
  2. Students complete the module's published practice exercises, using hints and feedback to improve.
  3. At the end of the module, the instructor administers an in-class variant targeting the same skills, but with different values, examples, or conditions.
  4. The instructor grades the in-class work for mathematical correctness, method, interpretation, and reasoning. Published practice scores are not the course grade.

Proposed grading allocation

ComponentAllocation
16 graded, instructor-administered in-class module variants80% total, 5% each
Integrated capstone investigation and report20%
Published formative practice exercisesUngraded; required preparation
Total100%

This is the proposed weighting for TeachReach and should be confirmed by the instructor before a formally graded offering. The in-class variant applies to Module 16 as well as Modules 01–15; the capstone is a separate integrative deliverable.

Typical in-class variant

Students may be asked to calculate or derive a result, modify a small piece of code, interpret a plot or probability, compare alternatives, or diagnose a failure. Assessment difficulty and permitted materials are determined by the instructor for the module.

Important distinction: Completing a website exercise or receiving a correct-answer message does not count as earning a graded module assessment score. The graded version is separately administered in class at the end of each module.

EXPECTATIONS & POLICIES

How to work responsibly

Academic integrity

Students should submit their own mathematical reasoning and code during graded in-class variants unless the instructor explicitly authorizes collaboration, notes, or AI-assisted tools. Practicing with outside resources is encouraged where allowed, but generated output must not replace understanding.

Assessment logistics

Assessment schedules, permitted resources, timing, make-up arrangements, submission methods, and any accommodations are communicated by the instructor. The public website does not automatically grade or record the in-class variant.

Accessibility and support

The portal supports responsive reading, keyboard navigation, text explanations, and browser narration where available. Students needing adjustments for an assessment should contact the instructor using the established course process.

Milestones and scope

Completing Modules 01–08 marks the mathematical and algorithmic foundations milestone. Modules 09–16 extend this toward modern ML and the capstone. Certificate or credit issuance, if offered, requires a separately announced policy; this syllabus does not itself confer university credit.
