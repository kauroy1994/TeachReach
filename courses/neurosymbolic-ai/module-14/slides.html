Comparing an observation with an intervention

Tell an observed group difference from the effect of a change, and calculate both in a small staffing model.

Before you begin

You can establish the order of two recorded events. You will now ask what would happen if the desk deliberately changed a decision.

Lesson outline

  1. Which shifts waited longer?
  2. Why was the extra clerk there?
  3. Change one decision inside a stated model
  4. Two explanations can fit the same log

Check your understanding

An extra clerk arrived at minute 12 and the queue shrank at minute 18. Which of these facts can you settle from the times alone: which event came first, or what made the queue shrink?

Reveal the answer

The clerk arrived first. Finding the cause would require information about other changes and what the queue would have done without extra help.

Which shifts waited longer?

Ava wants to know whether adding a clerk would shorten her wait for C1. First look at what the desk observed. An association is a pattern across observations. Here we compare wait times from two fictional shifts.

Worked example

On the quiet shift there was no extra clerk and the wait was 4 minutes. On the busy shift there was an extra clerk and the wait was 8 minutes. We measure the difference as extra minus no extra.

Show the Python example
wait_without_extra = 4
wait_with_extra = 8
print(wait_with_extra - wait_without_extra)
Reveal the output
4

Check your understanding

Complete the sentence using only the observations: “The shift with an extra clerk …”

Reveal the answer

… had a wait four minutes longer than the shift without one. The comparison changes both staffing and how busy the desk was.

Why was the extra clerk there?

The supervisor assigns extra help when a shift is busy. Crowding can also increase wait time. A factor that influences both the staffing decision and the wait is a possible confounder. The two observed shifts give us no equally busy pair with different staffing.

Worked example

Use 0 for quiet or no extra clerk and 1 for busy or an extra clerk. Each row records busy, extra and minutes waited. Grouping by busy shows which choices were actually observed together.

Show the Python example
shifts = [(0, 0, 4), (1, 1, 8)]
for busy in (0, 1):
    choices = [extra for load, extra, wait in shifts if load == busy]
    print(busy, choices)
Reveal the output
0 [0]
1 [1]

Check your understanding

Can we find out what happened on a busy shift without the extra clerk by filtering this table?

Reveal the answer

No row contains that combination. Filtering can select observations the desk has, but cannot supply the missing comparison.

Change one decision inside a stated model

An intervention sets a choice deliberately. To practice, assume the wait starts at 4 minutes, a busy desk adds 6 and an extra clerk saves 2, with everything else fixed. These are assumptions of a toy model. They let us calculate the outcome of each staffing choice on the same busy shift.

Worked example

Set busy to 1 in both calculations. Compare a clerk count setting of 1 (extra help) with 0 (usual staffing). The unobserved alternative for that shift is its counterfactual outcome under this model.

Show the Python example
def wait(busy, extra):
    return 4 + 6 * busy - 2 * extra

without = wait(1, 0)
with_extra = wait(1, 1)
print(without, with_extra, with_extra - without)
Reveal the output
10 8 -2

Check your understanding

Why does this difference have the opposite sign from the observed four-minute difference?

Reveal the answer

The observed comparison changed crowding and staffing together. Here the same busy shift is considered under two staffing choices. The model assigns a two-minute reduction to the extra clerk, giving 8 minus 10, or −2.

Two explanations can fit the same log

To see what the log leaves open, try another model. Suppose a busy shift adds 4 minutes and the extra clerk changes nothing. Both models reproduce the two observed waits. Their predictions disagree for a busy shift with usual staffing.

Worked example

Model A is the previous rule. Model B ignores staffing. Compare predictions on the observed rows and then on the combination the log lacks.

Show the Python example
def model_a(busy, extra):
    return 4 + 6 * busy - 2 * extra

def model_b(busy, extra):
    return 4 + 4 * busy

for busy, extra in [(0, 0), (1, 1), (1, 0)]:
    print(model_a(busy, extra), model_b(busy, extra))
Reveal the output
4 4
8 8
10 8

Check your understanding

Which additional staffing comparison would help distinguish these explanations?

Reveal the answer

Compare otherwise comparable busy shifts with and without an extra clerk. A study that randomly assigns extra help across many such shifts could reduce systematic differences between staffing groups.

Ask where two causal models disagree

Each function takes the same two inputs, desk load and the extra-clerk decision. For each input pair the loop runs both functions and prints their predictions side by side. The first two rows recreate the observations, while the last row compares the models on another staffing choice.

def model_a(busy, extra):
    return 4 + 6 * busy - 2 * extra

def model_b(busy, extra):
    return 4 + 4 * busy

for busy, extra in [(0, 0), (1, 1), (1, 0)]:
    print(model_a(busy, extra), model_b(busy, extra))
Reveal the output
4 4
8 8
10 8

Check your understanding

Replace this line: for busy, extra in [(0, 0), (1, 1), (1, 0)]: with: for busy, extra in [(0, 1)]: What will print?

Reveal the answer

2 4 On a quiet shift with the extra clerk, model A computes 4 + 0 − 2 = 2. Staffing does not enter model B’s equation, so it returns 4. These are model predictions for the proposed situation.

End-of-module questions

Question 1

Suppose usual staffing on a quiet shift gives a wait of 5 and extra staffing on a busy shift gives 7. Calculate the observed difference and name the question it leaves open.

Reveal the worked answer

The observed difference is 7 − 5 = 2 minutes. Both staffing and crowding changed. We still need to ask how the same kind of shift would do under the two staffing choices.

Question 2

Keep Model A’s starting wait at 4 and busy increment at 6. Change the assumed saving from an extra clerk to 3. For a busy shift, work out both waits and the effect of adding help.

Reveal the worked answer

Usual staffing gives 4 + 6 = 10 minutes. Extra help gives 10 − 3 = 7. Under the revised assumption, the intervention changes the wait by 7 − 10 = −3 minutes.

Question 3

A team evaluates a staffing policy by comparing every busy Friday with an extra clerk against every quiet Monday without one. Propose a more informative study.

Reveal the worked answer

For shifts eligible for either staffing level, group by expected demand and randomly assign extra help within each group. Compare waits measured the same way across many shifts. Record changes such as equipment outages that could affect interpretation.
